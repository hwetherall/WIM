Competitive balance measurement in sports economics literature relevant to WIM
==============================================================================

Framing WIM within established competitive balance concepts
-----------------------------------------------------------

Competitive balance research in sports economics has historically operationalised “balance” using **(i) dispersion of outcomes within a season** (typically win or points percentages) and **(ii) persistence/dominance across seasons** (repeat champions, stability of rankings), often motivated by the broader **uncertainty-of-outcome** idea that fans value contests whose outcomes are not predetermined.  Within that tradition, the most common “within-season” family of measures is standard-deviation based (e.g., the ratio of actual to “idealised” standard deviation), and many papers debate how well these measures actually control for season length, number of teams, home advantage, point systems, and tournament structures. 

Your **Wetherall Imbalance Measure (WIM)** deliberately targets something that much of the classic corpus does _not_ measure directly: **margin dominance** (structural separation revealed by scoring ratios), rather than **outcome predictability** (repeat winners and compressed win-percentage distributions). That distinction aligns with an increasingly explicit view that competitive balance is **multi-dimensional**, even if the field’s most common taxonomies define the dimensions differently (e.g., win dispersion vs persistence, or “static” vs “dynamic”). 

To position WIM convincingly (and safely in terms of novelty claims), the most important literature for you is:

*   papers that establish the economic logic for why “balance” matters; 
    
*   papers that formalise, criticise, or extend the standard win/points-dispersion measures; 
    
*   papers that explicitly propose _alternative_ indices (often inequality/concentration-based, sometimes entropy-based); 
    
*   papers that already attempt to quantify (or normalise) **game closeness / margin size**, which is the closest conceptual neighbour to WIM in the economics literature. 
    

Foundational papers establishing “competitive balance” and its economic role
----------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Paper****Simon Rottenberg (1956).** “The Baseball Players’ Labor Market.” _Journal of Political Economy_, 64(3), 242–258. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Rottenberg provides an early economic analysis of organised baseball’s labour market and the institutional constraints that shape talent allocation.  It is widely treated as a starting point for modern sports economics and for the idea that leagues have an economic stake in maintaining meaningful competition among teams. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is the citation referees may expect when you motivate _why_ imbalance matters economically (even if your metric is new). It supports your framing that leagues are “joint products” and that systematic dominance/weakness is not merely sporting trivia but part of the league’s economic design problem.  When introducing WIM, you can cite Rottenberg as the historical anchor and then argue that the measurement tradition that followed largely focused on win/points outcomes rather than scoring dominance.

**Paper****Walter C. Neale (1964).** “The Peculiar Economics of Professional Sports.” _The Quarterly Journal of Economics_, 78(1), 1–14. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Neale formalises the idea that sports leagues produce an “inverted joint product”: the value of a match depends on having viable opponents, so teams are simultaneously rivals and complements.  This logic underpins later discussions about why leagues regulate talent distribution, revenue sharing, and competitive conditions. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** Neale helps you justify why a margin-focused imbalance concept is economically meaningful: if the “joint product” requires plausible opposition, chronic blowouts and structural tier separation can degrade the product even if championship “surprises” occasionally occur.  This complements your “false equivalence” idea by grounding it in the economics of the league product rather than in any one metric.

**Paper****Rodney Fort & James Quirk (1995).** “Cross-Subsidization, Incentives, and Outcomes in Professional Team Sports Leagues.” _Journal of Economic Literature_, 33(3), 1265–1299. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** This widely-cited survey synthesises economic models of professional sports leagues as cartels and reviews institutional tools (revenue sharing, salary caps, etc.) and their incentives.  It argues that many cartel-management outcomes are consistent with league-wide objectives and evaluates which tools plausibly affect competitive balance. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is core for your policy implication: regulators may be optimising “balance” using particular proxies, and the effectiveness of tools can depend on what dimension is being targeted.  You can cite Fort & Quirk when motivating why a new imbalance measure matters for evaluating policies (salary caps, revenue sharing) and then use WIM to test whether policies change _margin dominance_ differently from win dispersion.

**Paper****Stefan Szymanski (2003).** “The Economic Design of Sporting Contests.” _Journal of Economic Literature_, 41(4), 1137–1187. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Szymanski reviews the economics literature on sporting contests through contest/tournament theory, linking incentive design to observed competitive properties in both individual and team sports.  The paper explicitly bridges “uncertainty of outcome” questions with contest-design and institutional structure. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is your high-status “bridge” citation that lets you position WIM as a measurement contribution consistent with modern contest-design thinking. It supports the claim that what we call “balance” is a design object with multiple facets, making your two-dimensional proposal (outcome predictability vs margin dominance) a natural extension rather than a redefinition. 

Competitive balance metrics canon and major methodological critiques you should engage
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Paper****Brad R. Humphreys (2002).** “Alternative Measures of Competitive Balance in Sports Leagues.” _Journal of Sports Economics_, 3(2), 133–148. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Humphreys argues that commonly used within-season measures do not capture important changes in relative standings over time and proposes the **Competitive Balance Ratio (CBR)** to reflect team-specific variation in winning percentage across seasons.  He also connects measurement choice to attendance modelling and reports that variation in CBR can explain attendance variation better than some alternatives in his setting. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This paper is useful for positioning: it legitimises the idea that the field’s conventional measures are incomplete and that alternative metrics can reveal economically relevant variation.  While Humphreys is focused on _dynamic standings mobility_ rather than scoring margins, you can cite him to support the general argument “one-dimensional measures miss something,” then argue WIM fills an _orthogonal_ gap: within-season **performance dominance by margin**, not year-to-year rank movement.

**Paper****Joshua Utt & Fort (2002).** “Pitfalls to Measuring Competitive Balance With Gini Coefficients.” _Journal of Sports Economics_, 3(4), 367–373. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Utt & Fort examine the use of **Gini coefficients** applied to league winning percentages as a within-season competitive balance measure and show that naïve applications can be misleading because league outcomes are mechanically interdependent (zero-sum structure in wins/losses within a league).  They propose adjustments and show that corrected Gini-based conclusions can differ substantially from earlier unadjusted estimates. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** Their core message—**structural constraints of league play matter for measurement validity**—is directly relevant when introducing WIM. You can cite them to pre-empt referee questions about whether WIM has hidden mechanical artefacts (e.g., how ties, schedule imbalance, or extreme low scoring affect distributions).  It also supports your argument that merely importing an appealing statistical index is not enough; its behaviour under league accounting identities must be understood.

**Paper****P. Dorian Owen, Michael Ryan & Clayton Weatherston (2007).** “Measuring Competitive Balance in Professional Team Sports Using the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index.” _Review of Industrial Organization_, 31(4), 289–302. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** This paper critiques the direct use of the **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)** for league wins by showing that the number of teams affects not only the lower bound but also the upper bound of HHI, making raw HHI values hard to compare across leagues/seasons with different N.  It derives an upper bound and proposes a normalised HHI-based measure applicable to wins. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is a “must-cite” methodological defence for cross-league comparisons: it shows how easily competitive balance rankings can be artefacts of scale (here: number of teams).  You can cite it while arguing that WIM’s ratio/log structure is designed to improve cross-context comparability, but you should also explicitly test whether WIM is sensitive to N (league size) in practice, analogous to the HHI issue. This paper also gives you a high-quality comparator when reviewers ask “why not just use HHI or normalised HHI?”

**Paper****Owen (2010).** “Limitations of the relative standard deviation of win percentages for measuring competitive balance in sports leagues.” _Economics Letters_, 109(1), 38–41. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Owen examines the **relative standard deviation (RSD)** (the popular win-percentage dispersion metric) and argues that, although it explicitly incorporates season length and number of teams, it does **not** necessarily “control for” these elements in the sense researchers often assume.  He highlights that the measure has an upper bound sensitive to league design parameters, and that accounting for this bound changes comparisons across leagues or over time. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** Your “false equivalent” examples are fundamentally about cross-league comparability of imbalance statements; Owen provides a direct, referee-friendly precedent for scepticism about standardisation claims.  Cite this when you argue that outcome-based dispersion measures can give misleading equivalences across leagues with different structures—and then argue WIM is designed to be robust to scoring scale (though you’ll still need to show robustness to schedule/points rules).

**Paper****Nicholas King & Owen (2015).** “Competitive Balance Measures in Sports Leagues: The Effects of Variation in Season Length.” _Economic Inquiry_, 53(1), 731–744. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** This paper analyses the distributional properties of RSD-style measures in the presence of different season lengths and compares them with other standard-deviation-based competitive balance measures.  The central point is that season design can materially affect competitive balance estimates, complicating comparisons across leagues and time. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is one of the most directly relevant “comparability” citations for your paper because WIM is explicitly marketed as scale-invariant across sports with radically different scoring levels. You can cite Owen & King when arguing that the literature recognises deep comparability problems (even within the SD-based family) and then justify why WIM is a different standardisation strategy. 

**Paper****Vani K. Borooah & John Mangan (2012).** “Measuring competitive balance in sports using generalized entropy with an application to English premier league football.” _Applied Economics_, 44(9), 1093–1102. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Borooah & Mangan propose a **Generalised Entropy (GE)** approach to competitive balance measurement, importing inequality methodology (entropy-based) from welfare economics and interpreting imbalance in terms of league welfare ideas.  They apply the measures to an _English Premier League_ season and discuss how imbalance can relate to within- and between-group variation among teams. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is arguably the most important “threat/support” paper for WIM’s intellectual lineage because it legitimises **entropy/log-based** inequality measurement inside competitive balance research.  You should cite it as evidence that the field _has_ used entropy/log structures, but then distinguish WIM sharply: GE is applied to **points/outcomes distributions**, whereas WIM is built from **scoring ratios (points for/against)** to measure _margin dominance_. This paper may also help you justify WIM‑TB conceptually: they explicitly discuss subgroup structure as a source of “paradox” (low overall balance, high within-group balance). 

**Paper****Kelly Goossens (2006).** “Competitive balance in European football: comparison by adapting measures: national measure of seasonal imbalance and Top 3.” _Rivista di Diritto ed Economia dello Sport_, 2(2), 77–122. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Goossens develops/adapts measures to compare competitive balance across European national top divisions, explicitly addressing the need for **international comparability**.  The paper introduces the **National Measure of Seasonal Imbalance (NAMSI)** and also uses “Top 3” style dominance metrics to capture concentration at the top end. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is a key comparator for your **WIM‑TB** variant: it is explicitly interested in top-end dominance rather than only whole-league dispersion.  It also provides a direct precedent for cross-league “apples-to-oranges” arguments in football contexts, which you can use while defending why a scale-invariant scoring-ratio measure is desirable. Finally, it is a useful citation to anticipate reviewer pushback like “top/bottom focus is arbitrary”—because “Top k” dominance diagnostics already have a lineage here.

**Paper****Bill Gerrard & Morten Kringstad (2022).** “The multi-dimensionality of competitive balance: evidence from European football.” _Sport, Business and Management: An International Journal_, 12(4), 382–402. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Gerrard & Kringstad propose a taxonomy of competitive balance measures and argue that empirical measures can be organised around at least two fundamental dimensions (in their framing, win dispersion and performance persistence).  They analyse European football leagues and examine how different measures relate (or fail to co-move), drawing implications for league regulation design. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is the highest-value “cover” citation for your central meta-claim that competitive balance is not one-dimensional.  However, it also potentially _challenges_ novelty if your paper claims to be first to argue multi-dimensionality. The safe positioning is: “multi-dimensionality is recognised, but existing dimensions are defined on win/points outcomes; WIM adds a **margin dominance** dimension based on scoring ratios.” You can also cite their regulatory-design motivation to reinforce your policy implication that targeting the wrong measure can misguide interventions. 

Scoring margins and dominance measures closest to WIM
-----------------------------------------------------

These are the papers you should treat as your closest neighbours (and, in a referee sense, your highest novelty-risk area), because they explicitly operationalise **closeness / goal-based dominance**, rather than only season-end win/points dispersion.

**Paper****Steven Salaga (2015).** “Competitive Balance in American College Football: The GI Bill, Grant-In-Aid and the College Football Association.” _Essays in Economic & Business History_, 33. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Salaga studies competitive balance in US college football in relation to historical institutional changes.  A key methodological contribution is introducing a **Margin of Victory Ratio (MVR)**—average margin of victory normalised by total points scored—arguing that “game closeness” measures were not widely used/available for American football in that line of work.  He interprets lower MVR as greater game-level uncertainty/closeness and reports long-run changes in that metric. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This paper is extremely important to cite because it establishes a _direct precedent_ in the economics literature for (a) treating **margin-based closeness** as its own measurement target and (b) using **a ratio normalisation** to handle scoring-scale changes over time.  Your positioning can be: WIM is a _season/team-level_ analogue that uses log scoring ratios (symmetry + multiplicative separation) rather than game-level margin/total-points ratios. If referees argue “margins have been measured already,” you can agree—citing Salaga—but argue WIM provides a different aggregation level and a log-ratio geometry that enables cross-sport comparison.

**Paper****Soudeep Deb (2022).** “A goal based index to analyze the competitive balance of a football league.” _Journal of Quantitative Analysis in Sports_, 18(3), 171–186. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Deb proposes a new framework and a **goal-based index** for competitive balance in football leagues, arguing that many earlier measures are adapted from standard economic theory but miss “finer nuances” of the sport.  The index is implemented on data from major European leagues and is presented as more suitable for analysing seasonal competitive balance and (in the paper’s framing) revenue variability. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This is a **high novelty-risk** neighbour: it is explicitly goal-based and explicitly claims existing measures miss sport-specific nuance.  You should read it closely and position WIM relative to it with concrete contrasts: WIM is not a “football-only” index, but a **scale-invariant scoring-ratio** metric intended for cross-sport use; WIM is team-season based and interpretable as average absolute dominance/weakness on a multiplicative scale. If Deb’s index is primarily about league-level balance in football, you can argue WIM generalises the scoring-input idea into a log-ratio framework that is comparable across low- and high-scoring sports.

Betting markets, ex ante uncertainty, and economic outcomes evidence you can use to validate “what WIM measures”
----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Your empirical claim that WIM correlates strongly with **Asian handicap** (spread) lines should be positioned against a longstanding practice of using betting markets to proxy expectations and uncertainty, especially in football.

**Paper****David Forrest & Rob Simmons (2002).** “Outcome uncertainty and attendance demand in sport: the case of English soccer.” _Journal of the Royal Statistical Society: Series D (The Statistician)_, 51(2), 229–241. **What it does (3–5 sentences).** Forrest & Simmons test whether match attendances respond to outcome uncertainty using English football data, and they derive an uncertainty measure from betting market information while accounting for biases in posted odds.  They report relationships between attendance, team quality, and their odds-based uncertainty proxy. **Why it is relevant to WIM.** This paper is central for your betting-market positioning: it legitimises the **use of market prices/odds as an information source** for uncertainty and competitive conditions in football.  You can cite it to justify why correlating WIM with handicap lines is a meaningful validation exercise (handicaps are an intuitive and market-tested representation of expected margin). At the same time, it can help you explain why WIM should not necessarily “beat” betting markets in match prediction—because markets are already aggregating rich information—while still being valuable as a descriptive _season-level_ dominance measure.

How to position WIM safely and persuasively against this literature
-------------------------------------------------------------------

The papers above collectively suggest a referee-safe positioning strategy:

First, claim continuity with the field’s established motivation: leagues are joint products whose economic value depends on meaningful competition.  Then state clearly that existing measurement taxonomies already accept multi-dimensionality, but they mainly partition dimensions within **win/points outcomes** (dispersion, persistence, prize concentration), not within-season **scoring dominance**. 

Second, emphasise that the measurement literature has repeatedly shown that “standard” indices can be fragile under changes in league design (teams, season length, bounds), which makes cross-league equivalence claims risky.  This is where your WIM pitch fits naturally: not “the old measures are wrong,” but “they measure a different object (outcome dispersion), and comparability claims are delicate.” 

Third, treat Salaga (2015) and Deb (2022) as your closest conceptual neighbours. Both already legitimise the idea that **margin/goal-based closeness** is a distinct measurement target—meaning WIM’s novelty should be framed as _a particular functional form and aggregation level_ (mean absolute log scoring ratios across teams; scalable across sports), not as “the first to consider margins.” 

Finally, when you discuss your betting-market validation, use Forrest & Simmons (2002) to justify market-derived proxies as credible validation targets and to contextualise why predictive dominance over betting markets is not the right benchmark for WIM. 

A concise “positioning paragraph” that fits journal expectations would read (conceptually): multi-dimensional competitive balance is recognised, standard measures focus on outcome distribution and persistence, and WIM contributes a complementary _margin-dominance_ dimension grounded in scoring ratios and validated against market-implied margin expectations—while being robust to scoring scale across sports.
