Adjacent fields for log‑ratio competitive balance metrics
=========================================================

Overview of the mathematical family resemblance
-----------------------------------------------

Your Wetherall Imbalance Measure (WIM) is structurally a **mean absolute log‑ratio**: it takes an entity‑level ratio (X\_i/Y\_i), maps it into log space where multiplicative gaps become additive, and then aggregates magnitudes via (|\\cdot|) and an average. A very close “adjacent” idea shows up repeatedly anywhere researchers want (i) **ratio‑scale, unit‑free comparisons**, (ii) **symmetry between “over” and “under” performance**, and (iii) **additive aggregation across units, time, or subgroups**—often for inequality, concentration/diversity, effect sizes, and “distance from a benchmark”. 

A helpful way to frame WIM in broader traditions is: **“a dispersion measure on log‑ratios”**. In several neighbouring literatures, the log transform is used explicitly because it makes ratio comparisons behave more “linearly”, treats numerator and denominator changes more even‑handedly, and often yields nicer sampling behaviour than raw ratios. 

Distributional inequality and decomposability traditions
--------------------------------------------------------

**Field: inequality measurement and welfare economics (Generalised Entropy / decomposable indices).****Approach/paper:** A standard inequality toolkit is the **Generalised Entropy (GE)** family (including the **mean log deviation** and **Theil** measures), designed around log terms such as (\\ln(y\_i/\\bar y)). A core reason GE measures remain central is their **additive decomposability**: overall inequality can be expressed as within‑group plus between‑group components under broad conditions, and classic axiomatic treatments single out GE‑type forms as uniquely compatible with families of desiderata (anonymity/symmetry over persons, scale invariance, population replication, transfers, decomposability). **Why it might be relevant:** WIM’s mechanics—log ratios plus additive aggregation—sit extremely close to the GE intuition: “how far is the system from an egalitarian (balanced) benchmark, on an information/log scale?”. If you want an **axiomatic scaffold** for properties like scale invariance and decomposability, the inequality literature (and its GE uniqueness discussions) is likely the most ready‑made framework to borrow, even though your objects are teams rather than households. 

**Field: official statistics & administrative measurement of inequality.****Approach/paper:** Policy‑facing documentation often describes the Theil family as an **“entropic distance” from an egalitarian state**, making explicit the bridge between inequality measurement and information‑theoretic “distance from uniformity”. **Why it might be relevant:** This language is unusually close to how a sports‑league metric is often justified: “how far is the league from balance?”. It gives you a precedent for framing a single‑number summary not merely as dispersion, but as a **distance from an idealised benchmark distribution**. 

Concentration and structural decomposition outside sports economics
-------------------------------------------------------------------

**Field: industrial diversification / regional economics (entropy as an alternative concentration lens).****Approach/paper:** A classic diversification line uses **entropy** over sector shares to quantify how diversified (vs concentrated) a region’s industry mix is, and emphasises that entropy can be **decomposed** into between‑set and within‑set components to analyse _where_ changes in diversification come from. **Why it might be relevant:** The conceptual mapping to your “outcome predictability vs margin dominance” distinction is: **“how many meaningful competitors exist?”** (variety/richness) versus **“how unevenly activity is distributed among them?”** (dominance/evenness). Also, the fact that entropy‑style measures were argued to support **structural decomposition** of change mirrors your use of additive log structure to decompose imbalance contributions. 

**Field: market concentration metrics beyond HHI (dominance indices; entropy‑family discussions).****Approach/paper:** Reviews of concentration measures catalogue families beyond the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), including indices explicitly motivated by dominance vs diversity considerations and the trade‑offs of different functional forms. **Why it might be relevant:** This gives you an “IO‑adjacent” precedent for arguing that **one‑number concentration** (like HHI) captures only part of market structure, and that alternative indices (often entropy‑connected) are used when one cares about _distributional shape_, not just squared shares. That rhetorical move parallels your critique of single‑dimension balance metrics that ignore scoreline dominance. 

**Field: energy / environmental economics & industrial ecology (index decomposition analysis; LMDI).****Approach/paper:** The **Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI)** approach decomposes changes in an aggregate into interpretable driver components using (i) **logarithmic changes** and (ii) **logarithmic‑mean weights** (L(x,y)=(y-x)/\\ln(y/x)). A major selling point is **“perfect decomposition”** (no residual term) and clear additive accounting of contributions. **Why it might be relevant:** Methodologically, LMDI is a strong adjacent precedent for your “log makes multiplicative structure additive” narrative: it shows how log‑based formulations are used specifically to enable **clean additive attribution** across drivers. If your paper needs a cross‑disciplinary example where log‑ratio structure is chosen because it enables **decomposition without residuals**, LMDI is one of the most cited non‑sports cases. 

Ecology, biodiversity, and the richness–evenness distinction
------------------------------------------------------------

**Field: ecology (biodiversity indices; dominance/evenness vs richness).****Approach/paper:** Classic biodiversity measurement distinguishes **richness** (how many species) from **evenness** (how equally abundant they are). Widely used indices (e.g., Shannon‑type entropy measures) use log terms over relative abundances, and a long literature debates which indices capture which ecological “dimension” of diversity. **Why it might be relevant:** This is one of the cleanest analogues to your conceptual split: **“predictability / concentration”** maps naturally to richness (effective number of meaningful competitors), while **“margin dominance / structural inequality”** maps to evenness/dominance (how skewed the abundance/impact distribution is). Ecology also provides language for arguing that two systems can have similar “counts of competitors” but very different “dominance intensity”. 

**Field: ecological critique and interpretability of entropy indices.****Approach/paper:** Work on interpreting entropy‑based diversity indices stresses that many popular indices are mathematically related (often through entropy families) yet differ in interpretability, and that “diversity” can be confounded with the index used. **Why it might be relevant:** This is a ready‑made precedent for a careful methodological section: it shows how another discipline has navigated a similar problem—many plausible scalar indices exist, but researchers must argue what _dimension_ of “balance” their index represents and why it is interpretable. That matches your need to defend WIM as measuring a specific “structural inequality in scoring” dimension rather than generic competitive unpredictability. 

Log‑ratio geometry in compositional data and omics
--------------------------------------------------

**Field: compositional data analysis (CoDA) in geoscience, microbiomes, and “parts‑of‑a‑whole” data.****Approach/paper:** CoDA treats data vectors whose components are constrained (e.g., proportions summing to 1) as living in **Aitchison geometry**, where only **ratios between components** carry information. Standard transformations like the **centred log‑ratio (clr)** and **isometric log‑ratio (ilr)** explicitly compute log ratios (e.g., (\\log(x\_i/g(x))), or for two parts (z=\\frac{1}{\\sqrt2}\\log(x\_1/x\_2))) to move data into ordinary Euclidean space for standard statistics. **Why it might be relevant:** CoDA is arguably the strongest **axiomatic precedent** for ratio‑based measurement: it formalises why ratio/scale invariance matters, why log‑ratios are the natural coordinates, and how distances/dispersion should be computed in log‑ratio space. WIM can be framed as a **league‑level dispersion functional on log‑ratio coordinates**, which resonates with CoDA’s “work in log‑ratio coordinates, then aggregate.” 

**Field: omics methods using log‑ratios as “within‑sample log‑fold differences”.****Approach/paper:** In “any‑omics” settings, log‑ratio transformations are explicitly described as producing variables that should be interpreted as **log‑fold differences relative to a reference** (often a geometric mean reference in clr). This positions log‑ratios as the basic unit of comparison, not raw counts. **Why it might be relevant:** Your WIM can be presented as taking exactly this kind of object—**within‑system (team‑level) fold differences of ‘for’ vs ‘against’**—and aggregating their magnitudes. The CoDA literature also gives you vocabulary to discuss “reference choice” analogues (e.g., conceded points vs league average conceded) if you ever generalise (Y\_i). 

**Field: genomics / transcriptomics (log fold‑change as a symmetric effect magnitude).****Approach/paper:** Differential expression workflows routinely use **log2 fold change** as the magnitude axis in a volcano plot, pairing it with significance (e.g., (-\\log\_{10} p)). Practical tools document that volcano plots show “magnitude of change (log2 fold change)” and even allow **absolute log2 fold‑change thresholds** (symmetrically selecting (\\ge \\tau) or (\\le -\\tau)). **Why it might be relevant:** WIM’s core term (|\\ln(X\_i/Y\_i)|) is mathematically the same object as “absolute log fold‑change”, just with a different substantive interpretation. This is a powerful “unexpected” citation bridge: you can point out that other high‑dimensional sciences treat (|\\log(\\text{ratio})|) as a standard, symmetric magnitude of imbalance, precisely because it is sign‑symmetric and scale‑free. 

Evidence synthesis and effect sizes built from log‑ratios
---------------------------------------------------------

**Field: meta‑analysis in ecology (log response ratio, lnRR).****Approach/paper:** A seminal _Ecology_ paper formalised the **log response ratio** (L=\\ln(\\bar X\_E/\\bar X\_C)) for meta‑analysis, explicitly motivating the log because it “linearises” ratio comparisons and yields more tractable sampling behaviour than the raw ratio. The paper derives approximate sampling properties and shows how to use log‑ratio effect sizes in standard meta‑analytic procedures. **Why it might be relevant:** This is very close to WIM at the atomic level: both use (\\ln(\\text{ratio})) because ratios are multiplicative and asymmetric on the raw scale, but become additive and better‑behaved in log space. If you want cross‑disciplinary precedent for **“log‑ratios are the natural unit of proportional dominance”**, lnRR is a clean citation. 

**Field: methodological refinements of log‑ratio effect sizes (bias correction; missing SDs).****Approach/paper:** Later work notes technical issues in lnRR estimation and proposes corrections or robust procedures (e.g., bias correction for log response ratios; strategies when standard deviations are missing). The point is that the lnRR family is mature enough to have a methods literature around inference, not just point measurement. **Why it might be relevant:** If WIM becomes a standard league‑level descriptor, you may eventually want an inferential layer (confidence intervals, sampling variation under schedule imbalance, etc.). The lnRR literature offers templates for how disciplines build inference around log‑ratio aggregates. 

**Field: medical statistics (log risk ratio / log odds ratio as standard analysis scales).****Approach/paper:** In evidence synthesis for binary outcomes, it is standard to analyse ratios on the **log scale** (e.g., log risk ratio or log odds ratio) because the log transforms multiplicative effects into additive ones and yields unbounded effect measures with more convenient statistical properties. Guidance documents discuss practical pitfalls and constraints but treat log‑transformed ratios as the basic meta‑analytic currency. **Why it might be relevant:** This provides a well‑recognised precedent for your **Z‑score normalisation** idea too: medicine often works with standardised effect measures and interprets them comparatively across contexts once they are on a comparable scale. More directly, it supports the argument that **log‑ratio magnitudes are the “right” geometry** when the underlying phenomenon is multiplicative (risk, rates, scoring intensity). 

Discrimination, fairness, and symmetric ratio disparities
---------------------------------------------------------

**Field: discrimination measurement and discrimination‑aware data mining.****Approach/paper:** Work on measuring discrimination in decision records catalogues ratio‑based measures (e.g., **risk ratio / relative risk** and related lifts), grounded in the idea that discrimination can be expressed as a ratio of adverse outcome rates between groups. A broader “discovery of discrimination” treatment similarly lists risk‑ratio and risk‑difference measures as canonical quantitative summaries. **Why it might be relevant:** Your symmetry point—(\\ln(2/1)=-\\ln(1/2))—is exactly what fairness auditing struggles with when using raw ratios: ratios (<1) and (>1) are not symmetric around a neutral point on the raw scale. The discrimination literature gives you adjacent motivation for moving to log space when comparing “advantage vs disadvantage” (even if particular papers use ratios more than logs). It also offers a close conceptual parallel: WIM is, in effect, a system‑wide average of symmetric “advantage ratios” (scored vs conceded) rather than group‑wise adverse‑outcome ratios. 

**Field: disparate‑impact methodology discussing log‑ratio approaches.****Approach/paper:** Methodological work explicitly contrasts difference, ratio, and **log‑ratio** methods for analysing disparate impact (at least at the level of abstract/accessible summaries). **Why it might be relevant:** Even if you do not rely on this literature for estimation details, it is a valuable rhetorical adjacent precedent: it shows that in another regulated, high‑stakes domain, researchers discuss log‑ratio methods precisely because ratios have awkward asymmetries and scaling issues. 

Information theory, statistical mechanics, and networks as competitive systems
------------------------------------------------------------------------------

**Field: information theory & statistical hypothesis testing (log‑likelihood ratios; KL divergence).****Approach/paper:** KL divergence is defined as an expected log‑ratio (D(q|p)=\\sum\_i q\_i \\log(q\_i/p\_i)) (discrete case), and it arises naturally from log‑likelihood ratio reasoning in hypothesis testing: log‑ratios quantify how much more plausible data are under one model than another. **Why it might be relevant:** There is an information‑theoretic way to “explain” WIM: each (\\ln(X\_i/Y\_i)) resembles a log‑likelihood ratio or log‑evidence of dominance for entity (i), and the league average of magnitudes is a kind of **system‑wide distance from balance in log‑evidence units**. While WIM is an L1‑style aggregation (absolute log) rather than KL’s expectation under a probability model, this section gives you a deep theoretical tradition for treating log‑ratios as the primitive quantity for “distance between states”. 

**Field: stochastic thermodynamics / statistical physics (entropy production as a log‑ratio of path probabilities).****Approach/paper:** A modern stochastic thermodynamics review defines a key functional for fluctuation theorems as the **log‑ratio of forward vs conjugate (often time‑reversed) path probabilities**, and interprets it physically as dissipation/entropy production. **Why it might be relevant:** This is an unexpectedly tight structural analogue to “for vs against”: competitive dominance can be thought of as **net directional flow** (scoring for vs scoring against), and physics formalises directional imbalance using log‑ratios of forward/backward trajectories. If you ever want a striking cross‑disciplinary comparison, stochastic thermodynamics provides a mathematically pure precedent for “imbalance = log ratio of opposing flows”, with symmetry under reversal built in. 

**Field: network science (centralisation/inequality of connectivity via Theil and relative entropy).****Approach/paper:** A network‑science paper defines a **Theil index for graphs** by applying the Theil functional to vertex characteristics (notably degree), showing it can measure how far the degree distribution is from uniform (regular graphs vs star graphs), and relates this to relative entropy and a “von Neumann Theil index” concept. **Why it might be relevant:** If you model leagues as interaction networks (teams as nodes; matches as edges; points/goal flows as weighted edges), then “competitive balance” becomes “how centralised are outcomes/flows?”. The network literature gives you both (i) a **ready mapping from Theil/entropy to centralisation**, and (ii) an explicit **benchmark comparison to uniformity**, matching your “balanced league” baseline logic. 

Competitive dynamics in finance, elections, and psychometrics
-------------------------------------------------------------

**Field: finance / financial econometrics (log returns; dispersion and volatility from log‑ratios).****Approach/paper:** Standard finance definitions set the one‑period log return as (r\_t=\\ln(P\_t/P\_{t-1})), emphasising that multi‑period log returns add over time. Empirically, some volatility modelling treats the **absolute log return** as a proxy for unobserved instantaneous volatility, modelling its mean structure to estimate variability. **Why it might be relevant:** WIM is essentially a cross‑sectional analogue of common volatility proxies: where finance studies time‑series dispersion of log‑ratios, you study cross‑team dispersion of log‑ratios. This gives you a well‑understood precedent for interpreting averages of (|\\ln(\\cdot)|) as “typical magnitude of imbalance” on a multiplicative scale, and for pairing it with standardisation (e.g., z‑scores) to compare regimes over time. 

**Field: political science (party fragmentation and electoral competitiveness; entropy, unpredictability vs contestability).****Approach/paper:** Party‑system fragmentation has been quantified using an **entropy measure**, treating fragmentation as related to the spread of vote shares across parties and examining dynamic adjustment towards equilibrium. Separately, recent work on electoral competitiveness explicitly separates competitiveness into **unpredictability** and **contestability**, reviewing indices for each dimension. **Why it might be relevant:** This is a direct conceptual cousin to your “outcome predictability vs margin dominance” framing: political science already distinguishes two “dimensions” of competition (unpredictability vs contestability), and uses entropy‑style measures of fragmentation as one axis. It offers both vocabulary and precedent for saying: **a system can be unpredictable without being evenly matched in margins**, and vice‑versa. 

**Field: psychometrics / educational and health measurement (Item Response Theory; logits as log‑odds ratios).****Approach/paper:** Introductory IRT expositions define a **logit** explicitly as a log transformation of a probability ratio (odds), and describe core IRT models as operating on log‑odds scales for endorsement/success. This creates a latent “ability” scale where differences add linearly, and “spread” can be interpreted through separation/variance concepts on that latent scale. **Why it might be relevant:** IRT offers a deep analogy for your (X\_i) vs (Y\_i) structure: it separates a person’s latent ability from item difficulty using a log‑ratio link, then studies the distribution of ability (and its spread) conditional on the measurement system. Conceptually, that is close to “team strength relative to opposition / costs”, and it provides precedent for (i) **log‑ratio links** as the natural modelling scale and (ii) comparing spreads via standardised scales across instruments—very similar in spirit to your z‑score normalisation across leagues.
